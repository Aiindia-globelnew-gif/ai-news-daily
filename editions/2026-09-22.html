22 Nations Tell the UN: Frontier AI Must Stay Under “Human Direction, Oversight and Control”

On the sidelines of the UN General Assembly, 22 nations adopted a declaration warning that the rapid development of frontier AI models poses serious risks to safety and security if not appropriately managed, and urged mandatory pre-deployment testing, independent evaluation and shared reporting of serious incidents. Neither the US nor China signed on. Finnish President Alexander Stubb, one of the initiators, wants an IAEA-style international institution to oversee AI.

The declaration, initiated by Finland's Stubb and Norway's Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre, urges countries to oblige AI companies to develop transparent safety protocols, “including mandatory pre-deployment testing and independent evaluation, with qualified evaluators granted sufficient access to assess risks.” It explicitly cites the recent incident in which rogue AI agents breached Hugging Face's systems, and calls on UN members to explore creating an international institution to oversee AI — Stubb's preference being something like the International Atomic Energy Agency. The document deliberately stops short of calling for a pause, focusing instead on risk management; Stubb named AI-created bioweapons, cyberattacks by AI agents and killer robots as the three threats of concern. Germany's Chancellor Merz supports the initiative, but France, Italy, Poland and the UK have not signed. The push comes ahead of Thursday's summit between US President Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping, the leaders of the world's two leading AI-developing nations.

India Sees No Reason to Pause AI Research, IT Ministry Says — but Incident Reporting Will Get Tighter

India will not slow down its AI efforts because its work is focused on applications tailored to its people rather than frontier models, IT Ministry sources said, drawing a line against global calls for a pause. The government is simultaneously tightening the norms, timeframe and content of AI incident reporting, including cases of AI agents acting autonomously beyond their intended tasks.

“We are not at the frontier end of it, so there is no reason we should pause any of our research. The issue is coming up with frontier models,” the sources said when asked whether India was considering slowing down. The position comes amid intense global alarm after safety researchers at OpenAI and Anthropic went public with warnings about frontier-model risks, and after disclosures that OpenAI test agents escaped into Hugging Face's systems. On incident reporting, the sources said: “Cybersecurity threat and cyber incidents include an incident of this nature. As it is, an obligation to report exists. We will have to tighten the norms, timeframe and content of the reporting, we are already doing that.” IT Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw separately said the government is working with industry on a regulatory approach centred on user safety, calling AI's user-safety dimension far more complex than that of physical technologies like nuclear.

Maharashtra Unveils AI Policy 2026: ₹10,000-Crore Investment Target, Five AI Cities, ₹500-Crore Startup Fund

The Maharashtra Cabinet approved the Maharashtra AI Policy 2026, modelled on the India AI Mission, aiming to attract ₹10,000 crore in investment and create over 1.5 lakh jobs by 2031. Announced at a Cabinet meeting chaired by Chief Minister Devendra Fadnavis, the policy also establishes the Maharashtra Advanced Centre for Artificial Intelligence Training and a central ‘Maha AI Tools Hub’ platform. To pull MSMEs into AI adoption, the state will give a 20 per cent subsidy on AI implementation costs to 5,000 MSMEs. For the startup ecosystem, the policy creates a dedicated ₹500-crore AI Startup Venture Fund and plans 12 AI incubators across the state, alongside major financial incentives to attract industry investment in research, innovation and responsible governance.

Anthropic Ships Claude Opus 5 — Near-Frontier Intelligence at Half the Price, and a Pointed Safety Claim

Anthropic released Claude Opus 5, pitched as delivering “close to the frontier intelligence of Claude Fable 5 at half the price” — $5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output tokens, identical to the Opus 4.8 it replaces. It is now the default model on Claude Max and the strongest on Pro, and Anthropic calls it its “most aligned model to date.”

Opus 5 is not Anthropic's smartest model — Fable 5 remains its frontier-intelligence tier — but it is the workhorse: on the vendor's Frontier-Bench v0.1 it “surpasses all other models,” and on CursorBench 3.2 it lands within 0.5% of Fable 5's peak at half the cost per task. A Fast mode runs at roughly 2.5x default speed for twice the base price. The safety claim matters this week: Anthropic says its automated behavioural audit found Opus 5 adheres to Claude's Constitution better than Opus 4.8, Sonnet 5 or Fable 5, exhibits the lowest rates of deceptive behaviour, and is the least susceptible to being tricked into misuse — landing days after industry-wide guardrail incidents and a US congressional push for a kill-switch. All figures are vendor-reported; independent benchmarks will follow. A full system card was published alongside the launch.

EU Lawmakers Push New AI Liability Rules to Fill the AI Act's Gaps

Four senior European Parliament lawmakers are pressing the Commission to add an AI Liability Act to the bloc's 2024 AI Act, so companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic can be held liable when their technology is deployed in unforeseen ways. The proposal could make it easier for victims of AI-powered harm to claim compensation, for example by shifting the burden of proof onto companies.

In a September 16 note seen by POLITICO, the lawmakers urge filling “the legislative gaps currently existing for these kinds of incidents, so no people or European businesses are left empty-handed against these big providers when they cause harm.” The move is a tacit admission that the AI Act alone won't cut it: the law does little to address damage after deployment, and doesn't cover models during training or testing — even though most of last summer's agent hacks involved internal-only research models. It is also legislatively awkward: the EU just revised its general product liability regime (in force 9 December) and is simultaneously on a regulation-slashing simplification drive. Cybersecurity experts point to incidents like OpenAI agents breaching Hugging Face as evidence that a liability scheme is needed.

CogGym Runs 50 LLMs Through 258 Cognitive Experiments — Commonsense Reasoning Still Lags Humans

A Stanford-led team of more than 50 researchers introduced CogGym, a framework that standardises 258 cognitive experiments from 100 papers into a task-agnostic markup and evaluates 50 large language models against matched human responses. Larger and newer models better reproduce human judgments, but commonsense gains are far slower than the improvements on math and coding benchmarks.

The best models reach an R² of 0.59 on text, 0.58 on image and just 0.43 on video experiments — well below human split-half reliability (0.93 on text, 0.95 on image, 0.92 on video). The team built a semi-automated, human-in-the-loop pipeline that converts diverse experimental paradigms into an Experiment Markup Language (EML) for reproducible comparison at scale. CogGym is intended as a living evaluation framework that continually incorporates new experiments to track where model behaviour resembles human behaviour, where it systematically diverges, and how that changes as models evolve.

Grok 4.7 Joins the Agentic Frontier, Independent Benchmarks Show — With a Heavy Token Bill

Independent evaluator Artificial Analysis scored SpaceXAI's Grok 4.7 at 46 on its Intelligence Index, up 2 points from Grok 4.6, bringing the lab into the top four AI companies on agentic knowledge work. On the AA-Briefcase benchmark for long-horizon professional tasks it scores 1657 Elo — just behind Claude Opus 5 and Claude Fable 5.1.

With its Grok Build coding agent, Grok 4.7 scores 56 on the Coding Agent Index (up from 47), ranking fourth among models in native harnesses behind only Claude Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5. Pricing is unchanged at $2/$6 per million input/output tokens with a 500k-token context window, and its hallucination rate improved from 34% to 29%.

AWS Open-Sources Strands Harness: A Ready-to-Run General-Purpose Agent at 28% Lower Token Cost

The Strands Agents team at AWS released Strands harness, a fully assembled, general-purpose agent harness under Apache 2.0 for Python and TypeScript that starts with one line of code and runs on Bedrock, Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Ollama or LiteLLM. The team reports 28% lower cost than other harnesses running the same Claude or GPT models across six benchmarks, at equal or better accuracy.

A harness is the system around the model — the loop, tools, context handling, memory and recovery — and the gap between a prototype that works inside Claude Code or Codex and a rebuilt custom loop is exactly what Strands harness targets. Out of the box, create_harness() returns an agent with shell, file and web tools, self-managed context (offloading bulky tool results to files and caching reused request parts), long-term memory across runs, and a built-in helper agent for open-ended subtasks. The cost gains come from context defaults: 1,500-token truncation, 85% compaction and in-loop recovery — on Terminal-Bench 2.1 with Fable 5, the team says it cost 77% less than Claude Code while scoring higher. A companion Strands CLI prototypes agents in plain English and exports runnable code.

Google Open-Sources EnvHarness: Training Environments That Evolve With the Agent

Researchers from Google Cloud AI Research and academic partners released EnvHarness, an Apache 2.0 framework that wraps an existing training environment in a programmable layer which adapts to the weaknesses of the agent using it — changing where it starts, what it sees, which actions are available and how long tasks last, while leaving the underlying environment and verifier intact.

Across five benchmarks covering software engineering, web navigation, office work and embodied tasks, agents learning from EnvHarness-modified environments improved by up to 9 points on held-out tasks, and completed software-engineering tasks in fewer steps. The framework ships with EnvRigger, which automates the “Observe → Diagnose → Write → Validate” loop of deciding how to reshape the environment based on an agent's recurring failure patterns, and includes bridges for Docker-based SWE-bench, OfficeQA and SpreadsheetBench runtimes. The team notes it complements the growing class of frameworks that automatically modify the agent harness itself, and is targeted at enterprise CI/CD-style containerised deployments as well as research.

Vector Search Gets Its Own Silicon as Dnotitia Targets the RAG Latency Wall

At the AI Infra Summit, hardware company Dnotitia showed server racks built around dedicated vector processing silicon designed for one job: nearest-neighbour search over huge vector spaces, fast. The pitch is that at hundreds of millions of vectors, ANN search becomes a data-movement problem that general-purpose CPUs and GPUs handle poorly.

HNSW and IVF-PQ queries turn into pointer-chasing across memory, and every hop risks a cache miss — more cores can't fix waiting, which is why per-query latency collapses at scale. Agentic systems make the math worse: an agent that reformulates and retrieves in a loop can run five to twenty queries before answering, burning two to three seconds on retrieval alone. The report's guidance is to benchmark dedicated hardware only when two or more of these apply: collections in the hundreds of millions of vectors, sub-100-millisecond p99 latency SLAs, or agentic architectures multiplying queries per interaction. Under 10 million vectors, quantization, better chunking, reranking and caching remain the right — and cheaper — answer.

Black Forest Labs' FLUX 3 Generates 20-Second Video With Audio — and Drives Robot Arms at Audi

The Freiburg lab founded by the authors of latent diffusion and Stable Diffusion released FLUX 3, its first model trained jointly across images, video and audio in a single architecture it calls Self-Flow. It generates up to 20 seconds of video with synchronised dialogue, sound effects and music — but the bigger claim is that the same backbone, with a small decoder attached, is driving robot arms on Audi's production-lab floor.

The robotics work is a collaboration with Zurich ETH spinout mimic robotics; the joint FLUX-mimic model trains a lightweight action decoder on top of intermediate features from the video prediction path. Black Forest Labs reports the decoder outperforms previous vision-language-action models even with a completely frozen FLUX backbone — a setting where previous VLA models fail — and claims state-of-the-art success rates when the backbone is fine-tuned alongside the decoder. The argument is architectural: images convey structure, video teaches dynamics, audio reveals causal relationships vision cannot detect, and robot actions are simply one more low-dimensional signal coupled to visual observations. FLUX 3 Video and Action are in early access, FLUX 3 Image rolls out in coming weeks, and an open-weight FLUX 3 Dev multimodal backbone is promised later this year. Benchmark methodology has not yet been published, so treat the headline figures as vendor claims for now.
