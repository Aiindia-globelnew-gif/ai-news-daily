US midterms hand AI regulation to a new Congress and a sceptical electorate

Tuesday’s US midterm elections put Democrats in control of Congress and, with them, a far less hands-off posture toward AI than vendors and enterprises have enjoyed. Voters also moved directly against data centres, approving restrictions or outright stops on development in many states and counties. Lawmakers are now promising sworn testimony from AI leaders, and a populist bloc is pushing for an outright pause on frontier development.

TechTarget reports that the shift is being felt on three fronts at once. Legislatively, a Democrat-controlled Congress is expected to diverge sharply from the federal approach taken under President Trump, with some members preparing investigations into AI chip export controls that could tighten rather than loosen them. Politically, the ballot results were blunt: of roughly 41 local measures on data centres, 33 were approved, according to Redbud Consulting, and Goldman Sachs estimates that moratoriums and delays now touch about 45% of planned US data-centre capacity growth. Rhetorically, the Overton window has moved — Abdul El Sayed, the newly elected US senator from Michigan, has called for a “full-on pause” on AI development, while US representative Yassamin Ansari of Arizona is campaigning against AI superintelligence. Kashyap Kompella, founder of RPA2AI Research, told TechTarget that boards should expect “greater scrutiny” of AI investments, and that companies will need to demonstrate governance and measurable returns rather than pilot counts. The practical read for enterprises is that state-level rules will fragment faster than federal ones, and that infrastructure timelines — not model capability — are now the most politically exposed part of an AI programme.

Sarvam launches Prism, an open-weight decision model that answers in 44 milliseconds

Sarvam AI has released Prism, an open-weight model built on its 105B-parameter Sarvam base and tuned not to write prose but to make narrow, structured calls inside enterprise workflows — routing a query to sales or support, flagging a document for review, or scoring whether an agent finished a task. Sarvam claims an average of 71.8 across three evaluation indices and a median 44ms response on an NVIDIA B200 GPU.

Inc42 reports that Prism places Sarvam alongside a small but growing field of Indian decision-model builders, including Kerala-based ConvAI Innovations with its Laya model and PurpleTalk’s Sthānika, as well as global labs now optimising for decision latency rather than generation quality. The pitch is architectural: most enterprise automation does not need a long chain of thought, it needs a fast, consistent, auditable yes-or-no with a confidence score, and a 44ms median is fast enough to sit inside a request path rather than behind a queue. Open weights matter commercially here because decision models handle customer data and internal policy, and buyers increasingly want them running inside their own perimeter. The claims are vendor-reported and have not been independently benchmarked, but the direction of travel is clear — Indian model builders are competing on deployment economics and language coverage rather than on frontier-scale pretraining.

Goa puts ₹250 crore behind a state AI policy aimed at 600 MSMEs

Goa has unveiled a ₹250 crore artificial intelligence policy to push AI adoption among businesses, expand school-level training and build a skilled workforce, as the state tries to diversify beyond tourism and hospitality. The policy targets support for startups and AI adoption at up to 600 MSMEs.

Business Standard reports that the policy, unveiled on 22 October, explicitly aims to make the tourism hotspot a serious player in the knowledge economy rather than a holiday destination with a startup poster. The design is unusual for an Indian state in that it leads with adoption and skills rather than with incentives for large capex: MSME adoption support, school-level AI training and workforce development, with startup support layered on top. That ordering matters because most Indian states have so far competed for data-centre investment and MoUs, which produces announcements but few durable jobs. Goa’s constraint is the opposite of most states’ — it has limited land and power for large compute, so a skills-and-adoption policy is a plausible fit rather than a slogan. Whether ₹250 crore is enough to move adoption at 600 MSMEs is an open question; comparable state skilling programmes have struggled with uptake.

India’s AI startups say the GPU bottleneck is now access, not affordability

Indian AI founders report that GPU deliveries are running three to four months behind, and that the binding constraint has shifted from being able to pay for compute to being able to get any at all. High-bandwidth memory supply is already committed well into next year, pushing some startups into multi-year capacity commitments they can ill afford.

Rediff.com carries a Business Standard report describing a market where allocation, not price, decides who trains. Founders describe being offered capacity on three-year commitments at a point when their revenue visibility runs to two or three quarters, and HBM supply — the real chokepoint behind GPU availability — is reportedly committed for the coming year. The practical effect is that Indian model builders increasingly rent inference abroad or fine-tune smaller models rather than pretrain, which shapes the kind of companies the ecosystem produces. It also raises the stakes on the IndiaAI Mission’s compute pillar: the government’s shared GPU pool is the main alternative to commercial allocation for a domestic startup, and its next tender has been repeatedly re-timed. The story is a useful corrective to launch-week narratives — India’s constraint in late 2026 is still physical infrastructure, not ambition.

Anthropic brings personal finance into Claude through a Plaid integration

Anthropic has partnered with Plaid to add a Personal Finance capability to Claude, letting subscribers connect accounts through Plaid’s read-only data links so the assistant can reason over balances, spending and holdings. The rollout is US-only for Pro, Max and Ultra tiers, and Anthropic says those conversations are excluded from model training.

Investing.com reports the launch as Anthropic’s most consumer-facing move yet, and the design choices are the interesting part. Access is read-only, which keeps Claude out of the transaction path and out of money-transmitter territory; the feature is gated to paid US tiers, which limits regulatory surface and support load; and Anthropic has committed that personal finance conversations are not used for training. The competitive logic is straightforward — personal finance is the highest-intent consumer use case in the category, and it is the ground on which Perplexity and ChatGPT have been fighting. The risk is equally clear: an assistant that aggregates someone’s full financial picture becomes a single high-value target, and Plaid’s link flow is the only thing standing between a prompt injection and an account summary. Indian users are not covered by this rollout, and no date has been given.

Targeted RL lifts a 12B-active open model by up to 18.6 points across 12 benchmarks

Surge AI trained NVIDIA’s Nemotron 3.5 Super VL — 120B total parameters, 12B active — on four reinforcement-learning datasets spanning software engineering, complex instruction-following, long-horizon tool use and multimodal knowledge work. Across 12 benchmarks the trained checkpoints gained 4.5 to 18.6 percentage points, reaching scores competitive with much larger frontier models.

The work, published on Surge’s blog, used Thinking Machines’ Tinker to run the post-training and produced a separate checkpoint per dataset rather than one blended model, which makes the result more useful than a single headline number: it shows which data mix buys which capability. The largest single jump came on a long-horizon enterprise workflow benchmark, roughly doubling from a low base, which is the regime where small active-parameter models usually fail. The broader signal is that the gap between efficient open models and frontier closed models is now being closed mostly in post-training, with curated RL environments doing more work per rupee than additional pretraining. That has real consequences for Indian teams: RL on a rented cluster against a 12B-active base is a tractable budget, whereas pretraining at that scale is not. The caveat is that the environments were built by the same company selling the training service.

Google open-sources an XLA serving runtime that skips the Python interpreter

Google has released the XLA Serving Engine under Apache 2.0, a general-purpose export and inference runtime for the OpenXLA ecosystem that lets teams export a model as an XLA SavedModel holding its StableHLO representation and execute it in C++. The pitch is a “train in Python, serve in C++” workflow for production environments where Python is not welcome.

The Google Open Source Blog reports throughput numbers that make the motivation concrete: for short requests, XSE delivered 2–5x the throughput of the best Python server Google could build, and 8–36x that of a straightforward one, while using less memory. The target is typically non-LLM workloads — recommenders, ranking, tabular and vision models — where per-request latency is dominated by interpreter and framework overhead rather than by matrix maths. Removing the Python interpreter from the serving path also simplifies the operational surface, which is often the real reason enterprises refuse a Python service in a latency-critical tier. For teams already on JAX or PyTorch with XLA backends, this is a relatively cheap win; for everyone else it is one more reason to keep the model graph exportable rather than fused to a framework-specific runtime.

NVIDIA widens its agent stack with a knowledge foundation and sandboxed tool runtime

NVIDIA has expanded its agent toolkit, adding an Agent Knowledge Foundation for grounded, permission-aware retrieval, a sandboxed tool-execution runtime, agent blueprints and new domain models covering world simulation and biology. The release positions the toolkit as an end-to-end path from data ingestion to a deployed agent with bounded tool access.

The update is best read as NVIDIA consolidating several previously separate threads into one developer surface: retrieval and grounding on one side, safe execution of agent tool calls on the other. The sandboxed runtime is the part worth watching, because unconstrained tool execution is where most agent deployments stall in review — security teams will accept an agent that can touch a shell only if the blast radius is defined by the platform rather than by the prompt. Linking a knowledge foundation to that runtime also addresses the failure mode that makes agents unreliable in production, which is confidently acting on stale or unauthorised context. For teams building on open weights, the practical question is lock-in: the toolkit is framework-agnostic on paper but optimised for NVIDIA hardware, which is fine for teams already renting it and a consideration for those trying to stay portable.

DeepMind’s EmbeddingGemma 2 puts text, video and audio in one vector space

Google DeepMind has released EmbeddingGemma 2, a 740M open multimodal embedding model under Apache 2.0 that maps text, code, images, video and audio into a single 768-dimensional space, replacing caption-then-embed pipelines. Modular encoders let teams load a 270M text-only variant up to the full 740M model.

Per the accompanying tech report, the model beats specialist and general embedders up to 12x larger on multimodal retrieval benchmarks. The engineering choices are what make it deployable: Matryoshka dimensions at 768, 512, 256 and 128 let one index serve multiple latency tiers, int4 quantisation brings active memory to roughly 567MB, and an 8K shared context accepts interleaved image, video and audio inputs in a single call. Code retrieval improved 14% over the first generation. The architectural point is that captioning was always a lossy bottleneck — a video became a paragraph before it became a vector, and everything the caption missed was gone. Embedding anything directly removes that step, which matters most for enterprise corpora full of screenshots, recorded calls and scanned documents. Running a 740M embedder on-premise is also a realistic option for Indian enterprises with data-residency constraints.

Box rebuilds enterprise search around NVIDIA’s Nemotron embeddings for agents

At its developer conference, Box unveiled Box Content Retrieval, which builds NVIDIA’s Nemotron embedding system into the core of its product so agents can answer queries from document metadata rather than reading whole files. NVIDIA expects queries answered four times faster, with 20% fewer errors and 90% fewer tokens.

TechCrunch reports that the feature is an implementation of NVIDIA’s Nemotron Embed project, which publishes open-weight embedding models aimed at retrieval — a category major labs have largely left to specialists. Box says the system will reach all Enterprise Advance customers with a rollout early next year. The token reduction is the commercially significant number: agentic retrieval fails on cost as often as on accuracy, because an agent that reads every candidate document burns context it cannot afford. NVIDIA’s VP of generative AI software, Kari Briski, told TechCrunch the gain comes from mapping concepts rather than keywords. Box’s advantage is not the model, which anyone can download — it is the permission model and metadata layered on top, which is exactly the part that is hard to rebuild and the part enterprises will pay for.

Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash tops both video leaderboards and cuts prices another 30%

Google has released Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash, which generates native 15-second clips at up to 4K with synchronised audio, and cut API pricing to about $0.07 per second of 720p video. It took the top spot on both the text-to-video and image-to-video leaderboards tracked by 36Kr’s report, ahead of Grok and Seedance.

The pricing move is the story as much as the quality: a further 30% cut puts generated video firmly inside the budget of routine marketing and product work rather than one-off campaigns. Google has also pushed the model into consumer surfaces, with free users getting three clips a month inside Google Vids, which is the distribution advantage OpenAI cannot match now that Sora has been retired. The technical step that matters is native audio — synchronised dialogue and effects generated in the same pass, rather than a silent clip scored afterwards. For Indian agencies and D2C brands, where short-form video volume is the core deliverable, a 30% cost cut plus free-tier distribution changes unit economics more than a leaderboard place does. Independent verification of the leaderboard claims is still thin.

GPT-Image 2.6 rolls out with native GIF animation and SVG export

OpenAI has completed the full rollout of GPT-Image 2.6, adding native animated GIF generation and editable SVG output alongside faster generation, with the quickest images arriving in about six seconds. The API now splits the model into a speed-and-typesetting variant and a quality-focused variant.

36Kr reports that the two-track API design is aimed at different jobs: one variant for layouts, diagrams and text-heavy assets where accuracy and legibility matter more than realism, and one for photographic quality. SVG export is the quieter but more consequential addition for design and marketing teams, because vector output means a generated asset can be edited in a real design tool instead of being traced or redrawn — the single biggest friction point in AI-generated brand assets. Native GIF animation covers the loop-and-reaction format that dominates messaging and social, which previously required stitching stills together. The roll-out also underscores how compressed image-model release cycles have become: version numbers now arrive monthly, and the differentiation is shifting from image quality to output format and workflow fit.
