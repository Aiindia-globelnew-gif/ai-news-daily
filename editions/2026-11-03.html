The New York Times sues Brave, calling it an “AI fence” for stolen news

The New York Times Company filed suit against Brave Software in Manhattan federal court on Monday, alleging the browser maker built an index of more than 30 billion web pages with an unidentified crawler, then sold access to AI companies it names as Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI and Cohere. The complaint also targets Brave’s Paywall blocker, a browser setting the publishers say lets users read paid articles without consent. A second suit from the New York Daily News, Chicago Tribune and Orlando Sentinel followed the same day. Brave has not yet responded in court.

The Times’ complaint, filed as 26-cv-9549, describes Brave as “a supplier, dealer, and distributor of stolen copyrighted content” and argues it “acts both like a burglar that misappropriates the content of publishers, and an AI ‘fence’ that sells stolen content to third parties.” It documents Ask Brave returning more than 400 words of a Times article verbatim on request, and reproducing one story at 1,291 of its 1,295 words through an LLM Context endpoint. The publishers also object to Brave blocking their ads while selling its own, and are seeking an injunction plus damages including ad revenue. Brave, founded in 2015, has raised about $250 million. The Times has separately sued OpenAI and Perplexity; Perplexity called the new allegations “disinformation”.

India is now ChatGPT’s largest market in the world, OpenAI says

OpenAI India managing director Prabhjeet Singh told The Economic Times that India has become ChatGPT’s biggest market by weekly active users, with the local base doubling since January. In his first media interview since joining in August, Singh said adoption has shifted from pilots and demos to production-scale deployment, and that UPI and payments announcements are expected in the coming weeks.

Singh framed the growth as depth rather than reach: “In less than a year, it’s doubling. It just gives you a sense of (how) people are beginning to find relevance and deep use cases.” He said OpenAI is engaging government agencies and enterprises on protecting critical infrastructure as autonomous agents spread, and expects “deeper localisation and investments in the ecosystem” across product, partnerships and community work.

Vultr opens a 20-MW AMD AI supercluster in Chennai

US cloud provider Vultr launched a 20-megawatt AI supercluster in Chennai on Monday, built on AMD Instinct MI355X GPUs and expected to hold around 10,000 GPUs in its first configuration. The company says it is its largest deployment in India and one of its two biggest AMD AI builds anywhere.

The facility is aimed at AI labs, startups, enterprises, universities and research institutions training or serving foundation models. Vultr plans to add AMD MI455X chips and Helios rack-scale architecture in 2027, and is targeting 600 MW of AI capacity in India by 2028 and gigawatt scale by 2029, without committing to firm dates. Chief marketing officer Kevin Cochrane said India is Vultr’s largest market outside the US and its largest footprint globally; the Chennai workforce could eventually reach 5,000. The company did not disclose the investment.

Google brings Dreambeans to India, its first market outside the US

Google Labs’ experimental Dreambeans app launched in India on Monday for Android and iOS, generating personalised illustrated story collections from the Google services a user opts into: Gmail, Photos, Calendar, Search, YouTube and Gemini. It supports 13 Indian languages alongside English, and is the app’s first market outside the United States.

Product manager Gozde Oznur said India was chosen for the volume of user requests and the unusually high overlap of Gemini, Gmail and Google Photos users there. Sensor Tower estimates put US downloads at nearly 55,000 since the June debut, 63% of them in September, before an 84% month-on-month fall to about 5,500 in October. Stories can now use Veo to animate some images, but users can pick only one language at a time and cannot mix languages. Google warns the app can hallucinate and asks users to flag bad stories.

NeuroDX, which is rebranding as Manaas AI, unveiled Sphota, a 240-million-parameter language model it says scored 52.4% across 11 benchmarks against 52.1% for a comparable Liquid AI model while using 50 times less training data and 4.5 times less memory for conversational context. Global

Anthropic starts watermarking four older Claude models today

From 3 November, Anthropic applies its invisible text watermark to output from Claude Mythos 5, Opus 4.7, Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6, with Amazon Bedrock following a few days later. The mark is a variant of Google DeepMind’s SynthID-Text: it adds no characters or tokens, costs nothing extra, and applies worldwide because Anthropic says it has no durable way to scope it by region.

Only vetted organisations — regulators, law enforcement, media, fact-checkers, independent researchers, educational bodies and EU civil society groups — can currently detect the mark, through an API in private preview; there is no public checker. Anthropic concedes the limits: detection is weak on short passages, sparser in code and factual text, and erased by a full rewrite, and a positive result cannot distinguish “Claude wrote this” from “Claude heavily edited this.” On Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6, changing temperature, top_p or top_k reportedly removes the mark. Generated files carry signed C2PA Content Credentials. OpenAI’s equivalent, textGrain, applies automatically only in Europe.

Source: The Independent, 2 Nov 2026

Policy

Europe’s 2 December deadline is 30 days out, and it carries 7% of turnover

Two new Article 5 prohibitions take effect on 2 December 2026 under the EU’s Digital Omnibus: AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery of identifiable people, and AI-generated child sexual abuse material. The same date ends the transition period for Article 50(2) machine-readable marking on systems already on the market before 2 August. Breaching Article 5 carries fines of up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher.

The prohibition is narrower than it first reads. Article 5(1a) catches a provider only where generating such material is the system’s intended purpose, or where its design and capabilities make it a reasonably foreseeable and reproducible outcome without significant modification and the system lacks reasonable and adequate safeguards. Deployers are caught only where they use a system for that purpose. The standard for “adequate” sits in Recital 12 — data cleaning, refusal training, output filters, abuse detection — not in the article, and the article says “reliably prevent” while the recital accepts measures that “sufficiently reduce” the likelihood. The penalty chapter has applied since August 2025; only the conduct it attaches to is new.

Research

Zyphra’s hierarchical attention matches dense models while reading 1% of the cache

Zyphra published Zyphra Hierarchical Attention, a sparse attention method that builds and searches a hierarchical tree to choose the relevant tokens for each query, reaching subquadratic complexity with suitable routing thresholds. A deep six-level tree scored 63.4% on LongBench v2 against 58.0% for dense attention while attending to roughly 1.1% of the KV cache during decoding.

On RULER-13 at 256K tokens, a wide-tree ZHA model reached 96.2% against 96.0% for the dense baseline, and a deeper tree reached 93.3% on a fraction of the cache. SWE-bench Verified came in at 67.4% for the deep tree and 68.4% for the wide tree against 67.4% dense. Existing pretrained models can be adapted by distillation on only a few billion tokens. Against multi-scale sparse attention the gap is widest on long-context retrieval: 93.3% versus 77.1% on RULER-13. Zyphra projects a 32–35x speedup over dense attention at ten million tokens and roughly 373–417x at a billion.

Research

HarnessMM cuts a coding agent’s token use by 88% and raises its success rate

A Tsinghua University and Microsoft Research Asia preprint reports HarnessMM, a recursive self-improvement method for agent harnesses, cutting DeepSeek Harness token use on HumanEval from 96.28k to 11.62k per task while lifting task success from 71.43% to 85.71%. On MBPP-sanitized, tokens per task fell from 53.98k to 10.61k as success edged up from 90.27% to 91.83%.

Efficiency gains usually cost accuracy, which makes the joint improvement the interesting part. Applied to Codex on HumanEval the effect was smaller but real: success held at 90.82% with 29.54% fewer tokens, suggesting the amount of redundant chatter depends on the harness. The paper, “HarnessMM: Recursive Self-Improvement for Harness Token Efficiency,” was submitted on 23 October by Yuhua Jiang, Chang Xu, Feifei Gao and Li Zhao. It is a preprint, and HumanEval and MBPP-sanitized are tidy benchmarks by design.

Frameworks

vLLM 0.33.0 speeds up speculative decoding and drops CUDA 12.9

vLLM shipped v0.33.0 with faster speculative decoding on both Nvidia and AMD hardware, an opt-in FLy policy that accepts some draft tokens with lossy verification for 1.04–1.31x decode throughput, and a new --monokernel decode path that gives 2.32x P50 interactivity at concurrency 1 on MI300X and MI325X.

Block-diffusion drafters no longer reserve draft slots, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash with DSpark on GB300 rose from 1,373 to 3,390 output tokens per second in the pull request’s benchmark. The breaking changes matter as much as the gains: CUDA 12.9 wheels are gone and builds are CUDA 13 only, Medusa and MLP speculators have been removed, and paused-generation requests now return HTTP 503. Teams serving large mixture-of-experts models get more tokens per GPU without changing models, but should read the release notes before upgrading.

RAG

A RAG study finds the index structure buys tokens, not accuracy

A paper from InfiniFlow and East China Normal University ran an agentic retrieval harness carrying only BM25 and grep, with no compiled index and no vector index, and reached 85% on BrowseComp-Plus and 91% on multi-hop FRAMES. Auditing four structural artefacts — summary trees, outline trees, an LLM wiki and an entity-relation graph — it found none improved accuracy by more than run-to-run variation.

What did move the numbers sat at the leaves, not the tree: navigation pages whose leaves hold atomic claims paired with verbatim evidence. Averaged over FRAMES, query tokens fell 38%, and one decision-gate arm cut tokens from 324,479 to 155,305 per query while latency dropped from 140 to 30 seconds. The paper also offers an arithmetic warning about deep hierarchies: an outline that routes correctly 95% of the time with a branching factor of eight lands near 75% end-to-end success at 10^5 documents. Its figures come from 200 FRAMES questions with no confidence intervals, and the authors call them measured operating points.

A former agency COO launches 39 marketing agents out of Gurugram

Sanjeev Jasani, a former agency group COO, and technologist Shubham Saurabh launched TurboAgents, an AI marketing platform built around 39 specialised agents across seven functions: strategy, creative, social media, ecommerce, video, SEO and productivity. The company is aiming at D2C brands, solo founders and boutique agencies that cannot staff a full marketing team.

The organising idea is Brand Brain, a central profile built by analysing a business’s website to capture brand identity, products, audience and competitive landscape, which every agent reads from so users do not re-brief each tool. A Workflow Builder chains agents into end-to-end processes that output marketing reports, ad creatives, ecommerce listings, short-form video and Google Ads campaign files. TurboAgents is available globally with a 48-hour free trial; plans run from $49 a month for Basic to $2,499 for Agency.
