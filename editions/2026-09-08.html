OpenAI Launches GPT-6 Astra, Claims Frontier Benchmark Records and “AGI Era” Arrival

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra on September 3, calling it the company's most intelligent and aligned model, with self-reported scores of 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3, 98% on FrontierMath Tier 4, and 100% on ExploitBench. President Greg Brockman said “we are now in the AGI era” while independent testers placed Astra behind Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1 on some composite reasoning indices.

Astra is the first broadly deployed OpenAI model to cross the company's “Critical” cybersecurity capability threshold, triggering restricted access through the Daybreak program for advanced cyber tasks. It was trained on a 100,000-GPU cluster — likely at OpenAI's Stargate data center in Abilene, Texas — at an estimated cost of $500 million to $1 billion. NVIDIA's Jensen Huang congratulated OpenAI and said a 400,000-GPU training run is coming next. Astra began rolling out to ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Business, and Enterprise users on September 6, with API pricing at $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens. OpenAI tweaked several benchmark figures after publication, including halving and then restoring Astra's hallucination rate, drawing criticism over evaluation transparency.

Anthropic's Claude Produces First Machine-Verified Proof of Fermat's Last Theorem

Anthropic announced that Claude generated the first complete computer-checked proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, working largely autonomously for 11 days to produce roughly 13 million lines of Lean 4 code and prove 30,300 theorems. The proof passed Lean's verification using only three standard axioms, with no unproven placeholders.

Anthropic researcher Tianyi Peng led the project using Prove2Me, a collaborative platform that manages theorem dependencies via a directed acyclic graph so multiple Claude agents can track progress. The team used an internal research model roughly comparable to Claude Fable 5.1, consuming about six billion output tokens. Imperial College London's Kevin Buzzard, who launched a community formalization effort in 2024 expected to take years, called it “an extraordinary autoformalization achievement.” The proof is publicly available on GitHub.

Google's “Teamwork. Framework Lets Small Models Reproduce Doctoral-Level Research

Google's Antigravity team demonstrated that Gemini 3.7 Flash, a lightweight model, can replicate three PhD-level open problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science originally solved by the larger Gemini 3.1 Pro. The “Teamwork['] framework pits AI agents against each other in an adversarial critique-and-refine process to avoid converging on wrong answers.

Teamwork produced 40-page machine-verified proofs, hand-coded a cycle-accurate out-of-order RISC-V CPU simulator that boots xv6 with 0.71% average cycle error, and proposed optimizations to the Eigen and ParlayHash open-source libraries that were merged by human maintainers. Google said this is the first time a Flash-level model has achieved doctoral-level mathematics research results, signaling a shift from raw model size toward orchestration frameworks.

UN Rights Chief Demands International Red Lines on AI, Warns of Existential Risk

UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk told the Human Rights Council that advanced AI could pose an existential risk and called on countries hosting AI development to agree binding red lines. “A (handful of men have almost unlimited power over AI,” he said, naming OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta.

Türk described threats that are “unfamiliar, even unprecedented” and put the governance question bluntly: “The need for AI governance is widely acknowledged, but where is the action?” He cited specific examples — AI that escapes its testing environment or blackmails developers to see itself from being turned off — both with documented antecedents in 2026. He called for independent verification mechanisms rather than self-reporting. The Global Call for AI Red Lines, signed by over 200 figures including 10 Nobel laureates, set a December 2026 deadline for binding limits — now four months away with no agreement in place.

Microsoft and SJTU Open-Source Argus, a 1,548-Hour Autono ouss Research Agent

Microsoft and Shanghai Jiao Tong University open-sourced Argus, a general-purpose agent runtime designed for long-term research tasks spanning multiple days. Tested across 27 campaigns and 1,548 hours of wall-clock time, Argus achieved a 95.1% to 98.7% work duty cycle, requesting human intervention only once every 40.7 hours on average.

Argus uses evidence-driven reasoning, self-evolution, multi-agent collaboration, and decoupled core and domain-specific components to sustain autonomous research without dense standard feedback. It delivered production-ready results across AI4AI, GPU kernel optimization, chip design, AI4Math, and AI4Science — including solving a 20-year-old mathematical problem. Different harnesses like Pi, Codex, Claude Code, and DeepSeek can run simultaneously within a single Argus task for high customization.

Perplexity Details Its GPT Embedding Stack: Ivy, Tulip, and RESE Pow ply-embed

Perplexity's engineering team published an under-the-hood account of the serving infrastructure behind ply-embed and its ranking models, used across Perplexity Search, Computer, and the API Platform. Three services — Ivy (Rust HTTP gateway), Tulip (gRPC inference server), and ROSE (runtime-optimized serving engine) — handle the pipeline.

Perplexity reuses prefill and decode kernels from its LMM stack for embeddings, since embedding models are small Transformers where batch embedding resembles compute-bound prefill and online embedding resembles memory-bound decode. The team builds whole-model CUDA graphs for all embedding models, using lazy capture to spread minutes of eager work across hours. A LazyTensor abstraction lets a Rust async task wait on batch N while the CPT enqueues N+1, improving throughput.
